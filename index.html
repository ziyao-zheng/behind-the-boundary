The central question

Who protects the people responsible for protecting others?

Behind the Boundary examines occupational psychological harm and the responsibilities of institutions whose workers face harassment, distressing material, and emotional demands.

Seven case studies across arts, media, archives, sports, and related professions explore how organizations recognize risks, provide support, and assess outcomes.

Where does the boundary fall?

Workers’ professional duties may expose them to harm. This project examines how institutions define their protective responsibilities and how employment arrangements affect access to support.

Look beneath the public-facing role

Public-facing work, content review, record preservation, and crisis response involve different psychological demands. The case studies examine those demands alongside organizational policies, professional guidance, and reported worker experiences.

Reading the evidence

Assessing protection requires evidence about access and outcomes. The analysis distinguishes organizational commitments, reported worker experiences, and gaps in the available research.

Cross-case analysis

Recognition — How risks become visible

TIFF’s 2024 controversy brought operational safety concerns into public view. The WNBA identified harassment as a league concern in its 2025 initiative. Meta’s statements acknowledge reviewer wellbeing. Archival and emergency-dispatch research examines risks arising in routine work. Björkenfeldt’s journalism research identifies difficulties in managers’ recognition of less visible psychological and social effects.

Public incidents, organizational statements, professional guidance, and research make different risks visible. Each calls for attention to who recognizes the harm, how it is described, and what institutional action follows.

Question for reflection What evidence or events lead an institution to recognize psychological harm as a workplace responsibility?

Access — Eligibility and use

TIFF’s reported benefits, Meta’s described counselling and breaks, and the WNBA’s announced security and mental-health measures identify forms of support. Volunteers, freelancers, outsourced contractors, and workers with limited scheduling flexibility may face different eligibility rules and practical barriers. The dispatcher and investigator cases leave access at particular workplaces unresolved.

Examining access requires evidence about eligibility, confidentiality, opportunities to use support, and any consequences workers anticipate when seeking help.

Question for reflection Who might be excluded from a protection that appears comprehensive in an organizational statement?

Prevention — Working conditions and exposure

A screening pause responds to an immediate safety concern. Security measures address threats, and counselling may support workers after distressing experiences. Moderation, archival work, dispatch, and investigation can involve repeated exposure within routine duties. Prevention therefore requires attention to staffing, workflows, exposure management, training, and supervision.

Immediate responses, recovery support, and changes to working conditions address different stages of occupational harm. The reviewed sources leave their effectiveness across professions unresolved.

Question for reflection Which institutional decisions could reduce exposure or improve working conditions before workers need support?

Responsibility — Employment boundaries and accountability

Meta sets moderation requirements, and contractors manage many moderators. Freelance journalists may lack employer-based support. TIFF volunteers and temporary staff may have different eligibility from permanent employees. In the WNBA, the players’ union represents workers in discussions about protection. Archives, dispatch centres, and investigative agencies shape the conditions under which workers meet their professional duties.

Contracts and employment arrangements distribute decisions about support. Analysis must identify which organization controls the relevant working conditions and how workers can hold it accountable.

Question for reflection When a worker’s duties cross institutional boundaries, who remains accountable for their wellbeing?

Effectiveness — Evidence of worker outcomes

Meta reports implementation of its commitments. TIFF’s wellness account describes general benefits, and WNBA reporting documents an announced initiative. Each leaves questions about worker outcomes. Guidance for journalism and archives recommends practice, with adoption across institutions unresolved. The dispatcher and investigator cases do not assess the effectiveness of a named employer’s protections.

Evaluating effectiveness requires tracing a policy through implementation, workers’ use of support, and outcomes. Insufficient evidence identifies a limit in that evaluation.

Question for reflection What worker accounts or institutional measures would establish that a protection is working?

What the comparison reveals

The seven cases connect professional duties with psychological risks and uneven access to care. Public visibility, repeated exposure, and crisis work create different demands. Employment status and contracting arrangements affect who provides support and who can use it.

Assessing institutional protection requires evidence about the conditions that create harm, workers’ access to support, and changes in their wellbeing. The cases identify documented responses and questions the available sources leave unresolved.

Reflection

Beyond the Boundary:
Rethinking Institutional Care

Use these questions to examine how institutions recognize psychological risks, provide accessible support, prevent harm, assign responsibility, and evaluate outcomes.

These prompts guide qualitative reflection. Their use as an assessment instrument has not been validated.

Recognition: Does an organization formally recognize psychological harm as an occupational risk?

Examine the risks a policy names, the workers it covers, and the procedures that follow from it.

Access: Can workers realistically access the protections being offered?

Consider eligibility, cost, confidentiality, time, and the practical barriers workers may face.

Prevention: Are protections preventive or primarily reactive?

Examine efforts to reduce foreseeable exposure and the support available after distressing work.

Responsibility: Who is responsible for protecting freelancers, contractors, and temporary workers?

Trace how employment and contracting arrangements assign responsibility and determine eligibility for support.

Effectiveness: How can institutions demonstrate that their protective measures are effective?

Identify evidence of changes in working conditions or wellbeing, workers’ role in evaluation, and unresolved outcomes.

Questions for further inquiry

Which questions can the reviewed sources answer? Consider what additional worker accounts, policy records, or outcome research would clarify the remaining uncertainties.

About the project

About the research.

Behind the Boundary is an independent study in arts management for students and emerging professionals in arts administration and organizational management.

Purpose & approach

The project examines psychological harm, harassment, and traumatic exposure across seven occupational contexts. It draws on secondary sources to assess institutional commitments, access to support, and evidence of outcomes. Unions, professional associations, and worker advocates are also intended readers.

A shared analytical framework supports comparison across differences in professional duties, employment relationships, and available evidence.

Two approaches to a case study

Incident-based research

Incident-based analysis examines specific events through their chronology, attributed accounts, organizational responses, and evidentiary limits. Reported allegations retain their status as allegations.

Occupation-based research

Occupation-based analysis examines recurring risks through academic research and other secondary sources. Study populations, methods, and limitations determine the scope of the findings.

Some cases combine incident reporting with research on the wider occupation.

Evidence & limitations

The source base includes academic studies, organizational accounts, investigative reporting, professional guidance, and informal accounts. Each provides a different basis for analysis. The case studies attribute claims and identify the limits of applying findings across populations, employers, and locations.

Source availability and reporting practices limit comparisons of implementation and effectiveness. An announced policy documents a commitment. Evidence about eligibility, use, and worker outcomes is needed to assess its operation. Gaps in that evidence leave outcomes unresolved.
